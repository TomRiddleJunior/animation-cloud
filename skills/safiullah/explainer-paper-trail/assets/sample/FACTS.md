# Fact sheet: "So you want to run a bubble?" (paper-trail sample)

Every number and claim on screen or in the VO, with its source. Checked on 2026-10-27.

| # | Claim (VO / screen) | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The South Sea Company was founded in 1711 ("Step one: a charter, 1711"). The charter shows its formal name, "Governor and Company of Merchants of Great Britain trading to the South Seas". | Correct. The company was "founded in January 1711". Robert Harley and John Blunt devised the scheme. | Wikipedia, *South Sea Company* (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Sea_Company) |
| 2 | "South Sea trade: a monopoly". The company had sole trading rights to the South Seas. | Correct. It held a monopoly on trade with South America and the "South Seas". | same |
| 3 | "It never pays." The trade-account sheet reads "profits were never significant" and lists the years 1714–1719 with dashes. | The claim is correct: the company "never realised any significant profit from its monopoly". The yearly rows are an illustration (the footer says so), not figures from a ledger. | same |
| 4 | "Swallow the nation's debt … £31 million, swapped for shares." The counter stops at £30,981,712. | Correct. The 1719–20 plan was to take over most of Britain's unconsolidated national debt (£30,981,712) in exchange for company shares. The proposal was accepted, slightly altered, in April 1720. | Wikipedia, *South Sea Bubble* / *South Sea Company* |
| 5 | "Higher price, bigger cut." | Correct. "The more the price rose in advance of conversion, the more the company would make." Debt was converted at market prices, and the company sold the surplus stock. The conversion card is a schematic diagram, not data. | Wikipedia, *South Sea Company* |
| 6 | "Hand ministers stock. Nothing paid. They sell it back high." The allotment list is generic ("a Minister", "a Duke"), with "names withheld" in the footer. | Correct in substance. Shares were "sold" to politicians, but instead of paying, the recipients held an option to sell them back to the company and collect the rise in price. "Huge bribes were given to politicians." The rows on the sheet are illustrative. No real person is named. | Wikipedia, *South Sea Company* |
| 7 | "The bill sails by." An Act page reads "for the South-Sea Company to take in the national debt · April 1720 · wording paraphrased". | Correct: the scheme was accepted in April 1720. The wording on the sheet is a paraphrase, as the footer states. | Wikipedia, *South Sea Company* |
| 8 | "January: £128 … by summer, £1,000." The chart shows Jan £128, Feb £175, Mar £330, end of May £550, early Jun £890, about £750, early Aug £1,000. | Correct. The price went "from £128 in January 1720, to £175 in February, £330 in March … £550 at the end of May … £890 in early June … at around £750 … reached £1,000 in early August 1720". The values between those points are interpolated. The chart is labelled "approximate". | Wikipedia, *South Sea Bubble* |
| 9 | "June's Bubble Act: no charter? Shut the gate." The page reads "June 1720 · no company without a royal charter". | Correct. The Royal Exchange and London Assurance Corporation Act 1719 (6 Geo. 1 c. 18), known as the Bubble Act, received royal assent on 11 June 1720. It forbade joint-stock companies not approved by royal charter. Recent scholarship and a *Journal of Economic History* article show that the South Sea Company pushed for it to shut out rivals competing for investors' capital. Other motives are also debated. | Wikipedia, *Bubble Act*; Harris, "The Bubble Act: its passage and its effects on business organization", *J. Econ. Hist.* 54 (1994); NY Fed *Liberty Street Economics*, "Crisis Chronicles: The South Sea Bubble of 1720" |
| 10 | Coffee-house handbills: "At Jonathan's, Exchange Alley", "At Garraway's". | Jonathan's and Garraway's in Exchange Alley were the main places where shares were dealt. The schemes printed on the handbills ("A New Fishery", "Improving of Land", "A Timber Trade") are invented and labelled "illustrative notice". | Grub Street Project (Change Alley; Garraway's); *London on the Ground*, "Coffee houses, Exchange Alley and the 18th century" |
| 11 | "September's end: £150." / "−85 %". | Correct. "By the end of September the stock had fallen to £150." (1,000 → 150 is −85 %.) The chart continues to about £100 by December: "back to £100 per share before the year was out". | Wikipedia, *South Sea Bubble* |
| 12 | "Newton reportedly lost £20,000." The note reads "is reported to have lost about £20,000 · accounts vary; one estimate ≥ £10,000". | This is the popular figure, so it is always said with "reportedly". Andrew Odlyzko (*Physics Today*; *Notes and Records* 2019) shows that Newton's net worth fell from just over £30,000 to about £20,000. Wikipedia: he "lost at least £10,000". The Oxford *Newton and the Mint* project: "supposedly lost a fortune of around £20,000 … the evidence is inconclusive". | Wikipedia; Odlyzko, *Physics Today*, "Isaac Newton and the perils of the financial South Sea"; newtonandthemint.history.ox.ac.uk |
| 13 | "He wrote gravity's law." | Correct: Newton's law of universal gravitation, *Principia* (1687). It is a joke line, not a claim about the crash. | common knowledge |
| 14 | "1721: the inquiry. The Chancellor's in the Tower." The report reads "House of Commons · 1721 · Report from the Committee of Secrecy". The card reads "John Aislabie, Chancellor of the Exchequer, expelled from the Commons, March 1721". | Correct. The Commons appointed a secret committee of inquiry, which reported in 1721 and revealed fraud and corruption. Aislabie had resigned the Exchequer in January 1721. In March he was found guilty of "the most notorious, dangerous and infamous corruption", expelled from the House, and imprisoned in the Tower of London. Aislabie is a historical figure (died 1742). No words are put in his mouth. | Wikipedia, *John Aislabie*; Wikipedia, *South Sea Company* |
| 15 | Avoided: the "company for carrying out an undertaking of great advantage, but nobody to know what it is" prospectus. | Wikipedia calls this story apocryphal (citing Odlyzko), so it is not used. | Wikipedia, *South Sea Bubble* |
